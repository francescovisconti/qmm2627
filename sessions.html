Sessions

Six two-hour sessions: lecture, discussion, and guided STATA lab

Readings marked as Optional are for further study and are not required for class discussion. The Applications are the studies discussed in class and used in the labs. Slides, do-files and data are added to each session as the course progresses.

Session 1 — Beyond control variables

Regression, identification and selection on observables

The course in one question: where does a credible comparison come from, and what must we believe for it to hold? What a regression coefficient actually estimates: potential outcomes, selection bias and identification as finding the right variation. Selection on observables and conditional independence. Causal diagrams as a tool for choosing controls: forks, chains and colliders, back doors and front doors; good and bad controls (mediators, colliders, and samples selected on them). The Table 2 fallacy: in a model built to identify one effect, the coefficients of the controls are not effects. Regression, matching and weighting as different estimators of the same assumption. When selection on observables is not credible: more similar units, falsification tests, sensitivity analysis, or a different source of variation (Sessions 3–5).

STATA lab: simulated data from a known causal diagram (university degree, cosmopolitan identity and EU support): four regressions (naive, with the confounder, with the mediator, on a sample selected on a collider) compared with the true effect; the Table 2 fallacy; the same assumption with different estimators, teffects ra and teffects ipw; balance with tebalance summarize.

Session 2 — Measuring concepts

From indices to confirmatory factor analysis, and measurement invariance

What it means to measure a concept well: from the background concept to the indicators and the scores, systematic and random error, and three ways to gather evidence of validity (Adcock & Collier 2001); the same indicator in different contexts. Why an index with a good alpha is not enough: reliability is not unidimensionality. Principal component factoring, mean indices and factor scores. The confirmatory model: the common factor model, EFA and CFA side by side, specification and estimation in Stata, the standardized solution, fit indices, modification indices and the risk of capitalising on chance; how to present and report a CFA. Comparing groups: configural, metric and scalar invariance in a multiple-group CFA (optional material, not covered in class).

STATA lab (Acock 2013, ch. 1): ten items on the role of government (NLSY97). alpha and factor, pcf before CFA; a one-factor CFA with sem, estat gof and estat mindices; a two-factor alternative. Optional, for home: a multiple-group CFA by gender with group() and estat ginvariant (Acock 2013, sec. 5.3).

Session 3 — The experimental benchmark

Experiments, survey experiments and conjoint designs

Randomization as the solution to the identification problem: why a lottery makes the selection term zero by design. What makes a design an experiment: Dunning’s three hallmarks, and the difference between true, natural and quasi-experiments. Where experiments happen, from control to realism: laboratory (Nugent 2020), lab-in-the-field (Grossman & Baldassarri 2012), survey and field experiments. Survey-experimental designs: anchoring vignettes (King et al. 2004), vignette experiments, list experiments, conjoint designs with AMCEs and marginal means (an energy-transition conjoint). Field experiments: what changes in a regression when the researcher assigns the treatment, balance checks and the role of covariates (Gerber, Green & Larimer 2008). Natural experiments with a true lottery (the Vietnam draft) and with as-if random assignment (Snow’s cholera study). Integrating qualitative methods and experiments: before, during and after the experiment; what the qualitative strand checks (treatment received, causal pathways, SUTVA, realism, causal-process observations); Paluck’s field experiment in eastern Congo.

STATA lab: anchoring vignettes on political efficacy in China and Mexico (ttest, tabstat); a list experiment in Afghanistan; AMCEs and marginal means from an immigration conjoint, with standard errors clustered by respondent (regress, margins); a field experiment on social pressure and turnout: the effect as a difference in means, balance checks, covariates and randomization inference with permute. For home: heterogeneity with interactions, margins and marginsplot.

Session 4 — From fixed effects to difference-in-differences

Fixed effects, DiD and parallel trends

Fixed effects as a research design: comparing units with themselves, so that whatever does not change cancels out, observed or not; two-way fixed effects. Difference-in-differences as an attempt to reconstruct the counterfactual that an experiment guarantees by design: Snow’s cholera data as the first DiD; the 2×2 table; three comparisons resting on three different assumptions (Card & Krueger 1994). The parallel trends assumption: what it implies, why it cannot be tested directly, and how prior trends, placebo tests and knowledge of the case make it credible. DiD as a regression with an interaction of two dummies. What can go wrong: compound treatments, anticipation, composition, spillovers, few treated units; staggered adoption only as a pointer. A published application: the boycott of The Sun in Merseyside after Hillsborough and Euroscepticism (Foos & Bischof 2022).

STATA lab: the minimum wage in New Jersey and Pennsylvania (Card & Krueger 1994): did the treatment happen; three comparisons and their assumptions; the same DiD estimate three ways (difference in changes, interaction in long data, two-way fixed effects with xtreg, fe); a control group the law did not touch as a placebo.

Session 5 — Regression discontinuity and instrumental variables

Finding a lottery at the cutoff, borrowing a randomizer

Two designs that use only the part of the treatment assigned by something outside the units’ choices. Regression discontinuity: a rule with a cutoff, and the comparison of units just above and just below it; continuity (nothing else changes at the cutoff) as the identifying assumption; the estimate as two regressions and one difference, with its uncertainty; placebo outcomes; strong internal validity, limited external validity. Instrumental variables: a back door that cannot be closed (schooling, ability and earnings); an instrument that moves the treatment and reaches the outcome only through it; first stage, reduced form and their ratio; two-stage least squares. The assumptions (relevance, independence, exclusion, monotonicity, SUTVA) and their epistemic status: only relevance can be checked in the data. What an instrument estimates: the effect for compliers (LATE), with a return to the draft lottery of Session 3. Weak instruments. Who invented instrumental variables. Two applications from political science: radical right success and mainstream party positions (Abou-Chadi & Krause 2020) and elite messages and support for European integration (Gabel & Scheve 2007). DiD, RDD and IV compared, and the role of knowledge of the case in all three.

STATA lab: RDD — do British MPs get rich in office? (Eggers & Hainmueller 2009): the picture first; the jump as the difference between two regressions; its confidence interval; a placebo outcome; Labour as an exercise. IV — a college nearby and the return to schooling (Card 1995): the OLS benchmark; the Wald estimate from four means; first stage and reduced form; ivregress 2sls and estat firststage.

Session 6 — From numbers to cases

Multi-method research, mechanisms and transparency

What a well-identified estimate still cannot tell us: the mechanism, the measurement, the assumptions and the scope. Integration, not triangulation: one method gives the answer, the other does a specific job for it. Six ways of combining methods: qualitative work first; nested analysis, with cases chosen from a regression (Lieberman 2005; Mattingly 2016); cases for measurement; cases for mechanisms and the tests of process tracing; cases for identifying assumptions, a recap of Sessions 3–5; numbers inside a case study. Common ways to get it wrong. Case selection after a regression: typical, diverse, extreme, deviant, influential, most similar and most different cases, and what each is good for (Seawright & Gerring 2008). Transparency in the quantitative and the qualitative strand, and in their combination. The course in one slide.

The session includes a workshop in which students present the design of their final paper.

STATA lab: a regression as a map of the cases. Life expectancy and income in 63 countries (sysuse lifeexp): fitted values, residuals, Cook’s distance and dfbeta; typical, deviant, influential and extreme cases; back to the large-N model with a variable suggested by a deviant case. Then the British candidates of Session 5: which biographies would you study, and why?
